The Movember Men's Health Guide

What to check, when to check it, and how to help a guy who's struggling. Read it, then send it to a man you care about.

Every November I put the razor away for Movember. This year it's personal. Mental health and suicide hit way too close to home for me recently, so this year I wanted to do more than grow a beard.

This is the guide I wish more guys had. It's short on purpose. I'm not a doctor, and nothing here replaces one. It's a checklist to get you talking to yours, plus what to do when a friend isn't okay.

1Check yourself for testicular cancer

Every guy, once a month. It takes two minutes.

It's most common in younger men, and it's one of the most treatable cancers when it's caught early. Most lumps are found by the guy himself.

  1. Do it in or right after a warm shower, when the skin is relaxed.
  2. Check one side at a time. Roll each testicle gently between your thumb and fingers.
  3. Feel for a hard lump, a change in size or shape, or anything that feels different from last month.
  4. The soft, tube-like part at the back (the epididymis) is normal. Learn what yours feels like so you notice a change.
See a doctor soon if you notice: a lump or swelling, a feeling of heaviness in the scrotum, a dull ache in the groin or lower belly, or any change that lasts more than two weeks. Don't wait it out.

2Talk to your doctor about prostate cancer

There's no self-check for this one. It starts with a conversation and a blood test called a PSA.

Early prostate cancer usually has no symptoms, which is why the timing matters. The American Cancer Society says to start the screening conversation at:

  • Age 50 if your risk is average.
  • Age 45 if you're Black, or your father or brother had prostate cancer before 65.
  • Age 40 if more than one close relative had it at an early age.
Tell your doctor about: peeing more often (especially at night), a weak or interrupted stream, trouble starting, blood in your urine or semen, or new pain in your hips, back or pelvis. These usually turn out to be something else, but get them checked.

3Get your colon screening

Starting at age 45. Yes, I need to book mine too.

Colon cancer screening is recommended starting at 45 for people at average risk. If a parent, brother or sister had colon cancer, ask your doctor about starting earlier.

  • Colonoscopy: every 10 years if it's normal. They can remove growths during the same visit.
  • At-home stool test (FIT): once a year, done at home. A positive result means you still need a colonoscopy.
  • Stool DNA test: every 1 to 3 years, also done at home.
Don't wait for your age if you notice: blood in your stool, a change in your bowel habits that lasts, belly pain that won't go away, or weight loss you can't explain.

4The yearly basics

Book one physical a year. Most guys skip it.

  • Blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar.
  • A skin check for any mole that's new, changing or bleeding.
  • Tell your doctor your family history: cancer, heart disease, diabetes, depression.
  • Be honest about drinking, sleep and stress. Your doctor has heard it all.

5If you're the one struggling

You don't have to have it figured out to reach out.

Feeling stuck, numb, angry, hopeless, or like everyone would be better off without you is a signal, not a weakness. Here's what to do:

  1. Tell one person. A friend, a brother, your partner. Say it plainly: "I'm not doing well."
  2. Call or text 988 any time, day or night. You can also text HOME to 741741 (Crisis Text Line). It's free and confidential.
  3. Tell your doctor. Depression and anxiety are treatable, and a doctor can get you started.
  4. Make your space safer. If you're having dark thoughts, ask someone to hold onto firearms or extra medication for a while.

6If someone you know is struggling

Most guys won't say it. You have to notice.

Warning signs: talking about being a burden or wanting to die, pulling away from friends, drinking or using more, giving things away, big mood swings, not sleeping, or a sudden calm after a hard stretch.

Movember teaches four steps, called ALEC:

  1. Ask. "You haven't seemed like yourself. How are you really doing?" If you're worried, ask straight out: "Are you thinking about suicide?" Asking does not put the idea in his head. It opens the door.
  2. Listen. Don't fix, don't lecture. Let him talk.
  3. Encourage action. Help him take one small step: call 988 together, book a doctor, talk to someone he trusts.
  4. Check in. Text him in a few days. Then again. That's what makes the difference.
If he's in danger right now: stay with him, keep firearms and pills away from him, and call 988 or 911.

Your November checklist

  • Do a testicular self-check this week.
  • Book your yearly physical.
  • Ask your doctor about a PSA test if you're 50 or older (45 if you're Black or a close relative had prostate cancer).
  • Book your colon screening if you're 45 or older.
  • Save 988 in your phone.
  • Text three guys and ask how they're really doing.
  • Send this guide to someone.
  • Grow the mustache, or make a donation.
