You're not alone

I just heard another story about a man who died by suicide, and it breaks my heart. Mental health can be so easy to ignore, especially for men. People can feel alone, overwhelmed, and convinced that the people they love would be better off without them.

As we get older, we get caught up in work and responsibilities, and sometimes we lose touch with the people who are closest to us. That's part of why No Shave November matters to me. It's a month to talk about men's health out loud, including the parts we usually keep to ourselves.

So here's what I want you to hear. You're not alone. Don't give up hope. Tell someone what's going on. Ask for help. You matter. Your life matters.

When the thought says they'd be better off without you

When you're in a lot of pain, your mind can tell you things that feel completely true. That you're a burden. That nothing will change. That the people you love would be better off without you.

That thought can feel real. It isn't the truth. It's the pain talking.

  • Pain narrows what you can see. It hides the people who would be crushed to lose you, and the ways things could get better.
  • Feelings this heavy do change. Even when it doesn't feel like it right now. People go through this and come out the other side every day.
  • Help works. Talking to someone, treatment, and support help people feel better and stay safe. You don't have to figure it out alone first.
  • You don't have to decide anything tonight. Just get through the next hour, and reach out to one person.

How to tell someone (without feeling ashamed)

Struggling isn't weakness, and asking for help isn't a burden. It's what you'd want someone you love to do. Most people feel honored to be trusted with it.

Who you can tell

Anyone you trust a little: a friend, a family member, your partner, your doctor, a faith leader, a coworker, a coach, an old friend you've lost touch with. Or a trained crisis counselor at 988, who doesn't know you and won't judge you.

Words you can send as-is

You don't need the perfect words. Tap Copy, paste it into a text, and hit send.

I haven't been okay lately. Can we talk?

I'm having a hard time and I don't want to be alone with it.

I've been having thoughts of not wanting to be here. I'm safe right now, but I need to talk.

Can you call me tonight? I just need to hear a friendly voice.

This is hard to say, but I'm struggling more than I've let on. Can we get together this week?

I don't need you to fix anything. I just need someone to listen.

Will you sit with me while I call 988? I don't want to do it alone.

Tips that make it easier

  • Text first if talking feels too hard. A text gets the door open. You can talk once they reply.
  • Tell them what you need. "Just listen." "Sit with me." "Help me call someone." People want to help. They just don't always know how.
  • Pick one person. You don't have to tell everyone. Start with one.
  • If the first person doesn't get it, try someone else. Their reaction is about them, not about whether you deserve help.
  • Be honest, even if it's messy. "I don't really know how to say this" is a perfectly good start.

Ways to get help

988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline

Call or text 988, or chat at chat.988lifeline.org. It's free and confidential, 24/7. You'll reach a trained crisis counselor who will check that you're safe, listen, help you work through what's going on, and share resources if you want them. You don't have to be suicidal to reach out. Overwhelmed, scared, or not sure what you need counts. You can also call for someone you're worried about. What to expect when you reach out.

Your doctor

Your primary care doctor is a good, private place to start. Say it plainly: "I've been struggling with my mental health and I need help." They can check for anything physical, talk through options, and refer you.

A therapist or counselor

  • Check your insurance company's website or the number on your card for in-network mental health providers.
  • Search the Psychology Today therapist directory by zip code, insurance, and cost.
  • Use FindTreatment.gov (from SAMHSA) to find mental health and substance use treatment near you, including places that offer lower-cost or sliding-scale care.

Your Employee Assistance Program (EAP)

Many employers offer an EAP with free, confidential counseling sessions. Look in your benefits portal or ask HR for the number. EAP counseling is confidential.

Support groups

NAMI runs free peer support groups for people living with mental health challenges and for their families. Find a NAMI support group. The NAMI HelpLine can also help you find resources: call 1-800-950-6264 or text "NAMI" to 62640, Monday to Friday, 10 AM to 10 PM ET. It isn't a crisis line. In a crisis, use 988.

More lines for specific needs

  • Crisis Text Line. Text HOME to 741741. Free, 24/7, with a trained volunteer crisis counselor. crisistextline.org
  • Veterans and service members. Veterans Crisis Line: dial 988 then press 1, text 838255, or chat online. You don't have to be enrolled in VA benefits.
  • LGBTQ+ young people. The Trevor Project: call 1-866-488-7386, text START to 678-678, or chat. Free, 24/7.
  • Trans and questioning people. Trans Lifeline peer support: 877-565-8860, Monday to Friday, 1 PM to 9 PM ET. translifeline.org
  • Drinking or drug use. SAMHSA National Helpline: 1-800-662-4357, free and confidential, 24/7, in English and Spanish, for treatment referrals and information. samhsa.gov
  • After a disaster. Disaster Distress Helpline: call or text 1-800-985-5990. samhsa.gov

Small things that help today

These won't fix everything. They can make the next few hours a little easier.

  • Reach out to one person. A text counts. So does 988.
  • Get outside, even for ten minutes. Daylight and fresh air help more than you'd think.
  • Sleep, eat something, drink water. Pain gets louder when your body is running on empty.
  • Move a little. A short walk is enough.
  • Go easy on alcohol and drugs. They can make dark feelings heavier and harder to manage.
  • Make your space safer. Put distance between you and anything you could use to hurt yourself. Ask someone you trust to hold onto things for a while, or stay with someone tonight.
  • Make a simple safety plan. Write down your warning signs, things that help you cope, people you can call, and the numbers above. Keep it in your phone. The Stanley-Brown Safety Plan is a good place to start.

If you're worried about someone

You don't have to be an expert to help. These five steps come from the #BeThe1To campaign, backed by the 988 Lifeline.

  1. Ask. It's okay to ask directly: "Are you thinking about suicide?" Asking doesn't put the idea in their head. Studies suggest talking about it openly can actually help.
  2. Be there. Show up, in person or on the phone. Listen more than you talk.
  3. Help keep them safe. Ask what's going on and help put distance between them and anything they could use to hurt themselves.
  4. Help them connect. Offer to call or text 988 together, or help them reach a doctor, counselor, or someone they trust.
  5. Follow up. Check in the next day and the next week. A short text matters: "Thinking about you. How are you today?"

What helps to say

  • "I'm glad you told me."
  • "You're not a burden. I want to help."
  • "I'm not going anywhere."
  • "Can we call 988 together?"

What to avoid

  • Arguing, lecturing, or telling them how lucky they are.
  • Promising to keep it a secret. Their safety comes first.
  • Trying to handle it all alone. You can call 988 for advice too.
If someone is in immediate danger, call 911. Stay with them until help arrives if it's safe for you to do so.
